On the Traverse / Case study

Finding the right mountain is half the adventure.

There are plenty of ways to choose where to ski. Snowfall totals, vertical feet, lift tickets, the promise of a decent après. None tells the whole story.

On the Traverse is a guide to skiing the Northeast, built to help people find mountains that suit the way they actually ski. It brings together mountain facts, local knowledge, thoughtful comparisons, and a little help from AI.

The problem

Bigger isn’t always better.

The Northeast has its share of famous mountains. It also has modest hills with loyal followings, excellent terrain tucked away in unlikely places, and ski areas that haven’t changed much in decades.

Choosing between them isn’t simply a matter of counting trails.

A family heading north from New York has different priorities than someone looking for steep glades and a quiet midweek escape. A mountain that’s perfect after a storm might be less appealing on a crowded Saturday.

Most resort websites are very good at telling you why you should visit. They’re less helpful when you’re trying to decide between them.

I wanted to make that decision easier. Not by declaring one mountain better than another, but by helping people understand what makes each worth visiting.

The foundation

Every mountain has its own story. The facts should still agree.

Before designing the experience, I needed a consistent way to describe a mountain.

Vertical drop, snowfall, terrain, lift access, driving distance, and the price of a day on snow all matter. So do the less measurable qualities: atmosphere, tree skiing, family friendliness, and whether there’s somewhere worth stopping afterward.

I organized this information in Google Sheets and TypeScript, creating a shared foundation for the entire product.

The distinction between fact and opinion was deliberate. Snowfall can be measured. Atmosphere requires judgment. Missing information shouldn’t be mistaken for a zero. Experience ratings remain subjective assessments; some are prototypes, rather than verified mountain facts.

The structure also lets ordinary catalog content change without rebuilding the application.

The aim is a guide that can compare mountains consistently without pretending they’re all alike.

The experience

A little browsing. A little comparing. Then a decision.

Finding somewhere to ski should be enjoyable. It shouldn’t require opening a dozen browser tabs.

I designed the experience around three familiar steps: explore, compare, and look more closely. I built them in Next.js and TypeScript, using shared cards, metadata, selection controls, and a consistent information model. The aim was to make moving between browsing, comparing, and deciding feel natural.

Designing with AI

Skiers don’t always think in filters.

Someone planning a weekend might know exactly what they’re after without knowing which boxes to check.

“Good après, close to Saratoga.”

It’s a perfectly reasonable request. It doesn’t fit neatly into a traditional search form.

I built a natural-language search experience that interprets requests like this and translates them into criteria the product understands.

The important decision was what happened next. Rather than asking an AI model to recommend mountains from memory, I separated interpretation from selection.

  2. 02 / Constrain

    Check the facts

    Application code searches the loaded catalog and applies explicit constraints to identify which mountains qualify. Unsupported criteria and missing information remain visible.

  3. 03 / Rank

    Make a recommendation

    Supported preferences determine the ranking in deterministic code. The product generates reasons from matched catalog attributes.

AI recommendations

A recommendation should have a reason.

AI can be persuasive even when it doesn’t know what it’s talking about.

I didn’t want a confident recommendation for a mountain that doesn’t exist, a bar that isn’t open, or snow conditions that haven’t been verified.

The model interprets what someone wants. The catalog establishes what the product knows. The application makes the selection.

There are limits, of course. A request for a particular beer on tap requires information the guide doesn’t have. So does a question about live conditions. Those limits should be visible rather than quietly filled in with guesses.

A useful recommendation isn’t necessarily the most confident one. It’s the one you can understand.

The trust boundary

The model does not supply resort facts or choose mountains from its own knowledge. A match score describes heuristic fit; it is not a probability that someone will enjoy a trip.

Designing the limits is part of the feature.

Unsupported requests

Show an unverified state when the product cannot support a criterion. Exact drink availability and live conditions require evidence outside this model.

Geographic ambiguity

Distinguish location proximity, straight-line distance and stored NYC drive time. They answer different questions.

From design to code

The little things are rarely little.

A design still has to survive a phone screen, a slow connection, and someone changing their mind halfway through a search.

Building the product myself brought those details into focus. Source reviews and design audits helped me identify where consistency and interaction behavior needed closer attention.

01

Put the question first

On mobile, I brought natural-language search closer to the beginning of the experience, while keeping traditional browsing controls available. Both ways of finding a mountain should feel equally natural.

02

Make every action clear

Sorting, filtering, comparing, and opening a mountain’s details are different tasks. Their controls should behave accordingly, especially when someone has already selected a few places.

03

Don’t lose your place

Returning from a mountain’s details shouldn’t mean starting the search again. Selection states, touch targets, dialog focus, scroll restoration, and loading or empty states deserve as much attention as the main features. These remain areas of refinement, with physical-device testing planned.

What I learned

Good recommendations begin long before the search.

The most interesting part of this project has been deciding what information deserves to be trusted, how different kinds of information should be compared, and what to do when the answer isn’t certain.

The work has brought together product design, editorial judgment, information architecture, and application development. It has also reinforced something I believe about useful technology: the complexity should belong to the product, not the person using it.

The next step is to put the experience in front of skiers and see what happens.

What this work demonstrates

  • Product design carried into application code.
  • A shared data model across discovery and comparison.
  • An explicit boundary between AI interpretation and recommendation logic.

What I would validate next

  • Can skiers explain why a mountain matches their request?
  • Can they compare and return to browsing without losing context?
  • Do unsupported criteria and unknown facts remain clear?

Those questions still need real-world testing. Improvements in usability, conversion, and retention haven’t yet been established.

The goal is straightforward: less time sorting through websites, more confidence choosing where to go.

And, with any luck, more time skiing.
