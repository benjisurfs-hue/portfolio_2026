Travel insurance / Case study

From Quote Forms to Conversations

Designing an AI-powered travel insurance shopping experience

01 Summary02 Problem03 What we learned04 Key decision05 Design06 Outcome07 Reflection

01 / Summary

The next question: how could conversational AI help travelers understand coverage in the context of their plans?

The work spans six design boards: problem framing, conversation architecture, interaction scripts, a component system, an editable prototype, and an evaluation framework. Together they explore a decision-making experience that gathers context progressively, explains coverage, and handles uncertainty.

02 / Problem

A quote form can capture a destination, dates, age, and trip cost. It leaves a harder question unanswered: does this policy cover the trip I actually intend to take?

03 / What we learned

The design hypothesis was that travelers can describe their needs naturally, then review a structured summary before comparing plans. The test described in the Outcome checks that hypothesis.

The conversation architecture and scripts turn that hypothesis into interaction rules: clarify progressively, make assumptions visible, and give travelers a way to correct them. The experience needs to recover when the assistant misunderstands, as well as explain when coverage remains uncertain.

04 / Key decision

Use conversation to understand the trip, then preserve explicit comparison and review. Gather enough context to ask the next useful question, explain why it matters, and let the traveler correct the summary before moving on. Travelers can still compare all three plans and inspect benefits, exclusions, and evidence.

The critical trade-off is confidence versus certainty. A recommendation should explain its reasoning without presenting unresolved coverage as verified. The concept labels missing checks, requires policy evidence, and blocks a real handoff until the required checks and consent are complete.

The assistant is built to recognize the edge of its knowledge. In testing, a traveler asked about backcountry skiing without a guide. The assistant did not assert coverage. It answered: “Neither plan’s backcountry eligibility has been verified. Check terrain, activity exclusions and rescue provisions in the policy wording.” I also probed it with unrelated questions to check that it stayed within scope.

06 / Outcome

Method. Eleven real travelers completed a quote task remotely through Hotjar. Each used both the existing Soventure quote form and the conversational concept. The concept ran on a live language model that I built and fed with real PDS documents. I logged every message typed and every response. After each flow, participants rated their confidence in their policy choice on a five-point scale: not very, a little, so-so, confident, no doubt.

Speed. Quote completion time fell from about 7 minutes with the quote form to about 3 minutes with the conversational concept, a reduction of roughly 50–60%.

Confidence. The same 11 participants rated each flow:

Participants said that being able to ask questions directly left less ambiguity than reading generic summaries.

Uncertainty handling. The assistant is designed to say when coverage is unverified instead of guessing. For the backcountry skiing question described in Key decision, it declined to assert eligibility and sent the traveler to the policy wording. The evaluation framework below covers context accuracy, trust, and comprehension.

Limits. The sample is small and was tested remotely, so these results are directional rather than statistically significant. Confidence is self-reported: it shows how sure travelers felt, not whether the policy they chose fit their trip.

Next. Test comprehension directly: whether travelers can identify activity exclusions, distinguish unverified terms from verified coverage, and explain why they chose a plan. Repeat with a larger sample, and test quote failures, expiry, changed terms, and consent renewal in a full integration. Review the verification workflow with insurance specialists.

07 / Reflection

The central design challenge is helping someone make a decision while their needs and the available evidence are still becoming clear. Conversation can make a complex purchase feel approachable, but the design must also make uncertainty visible. The most important moment may be the point where the assistant pauses and asks for verification.

The test suggests conversation can make a complex purchase faster and less ambiguous, provided the assistant admits what it does not know. The harder question is whether travelers understand what they are buying, so comprehension is the next test, alongside review of the verification workflow with insurance specialists. The goal is an informed choice supported by exact policy wording, with the traveler in control of what is shared and when to continue.
